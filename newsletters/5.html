Papers and Projects Using OSCAR:

  • Annalisa Grossi and Stevell Muller, in their Corrigendum to "Nonsymplectic automorphisms of prime order on O'Grady's sixfolds", correct the lattice theoretic classification in a previous paper of Grossi's, by including some missing cases and explaining how to recover them. They used OSCAR to compute and correct their original tables listing, among others, invariant and coinvariant sublattices of effective nonsymplectic groups G ⊂ O(L) on manifolds of O'Grady's 6-dimensional deformation type.
  • Jie Fu, in his preprint Remarks on Lattice Actions Corresponding to Automorphism Groups of Smooth Cubic Fourfolds, completes the calculation of group actions on the primitive integral cohomology lattice for all of the 156 families in the previously known classification. He distinguishes similar families, determines the remaining action-maximal cases, and uses restriction to complete the list. The generic algebraic lattices for each family are also determined. The calculations mainly depend on OSCAR.
  • Simon Brandhorst and Tommy Hofmann, in their preprint Automorphisms and canonical forms of integer lattices, improve the Piesken–Souvignier algorithm for computing automorphism groups and isometries of positive definite integer quadratic lattices by exploiting characteristic sublattices and refining vector enumeration. For lattices with roots, they also use the Weyl group to accelerate the computation of canonical forms. In experiments with concrete implementations in OSCAR, they demonstrate substantial speedups and show that canonical forms can be computed at a cost comparable to that of computing automorphism groups.

Top 5 Secret OSCAR Tricks

Here is a Top 5 of perhaps not so well-known OSCAR hacks that might save you a lot of time. Thanks to everyone for contributing their favorite tricks!

  1. To multiply two matrices that both contain many zero entries, it can be beneficial to convert them to sparse_matrix and then multiply them using mul_sparse. The final result can be converted back to a (dense) matrix by calling matrix(...). For instance, M1 = sparse_matrix(M1); M2 = sparse_matrix(M2); matrix(sparse_mul(M1, M2)).
  2. Oscar.flatten lets you turn a nested ring like ℚ[x, y][s, t] into the isomorphic flat ring ℚ[x, y, s, t]. For example, given Qxy, (x, y) = QQ[:x, :y]; R, (s, t) = Qxy[:s, :t];, you can call phi = Oscar.flatten(R) to obtain the canonical isomorphism ℚ[x, y][s, t] → ℚ[x, y, s, t]. This enables natural modeling of parameters vs. variables and then switching to the flat representation required e.g. by Gröbner bases and elimination.
  3. Enter $ within an OSCAR shell to activate a polymake session. You can even exchange variables between polymake and OSCAR!
  4. In the very same vein, you can type GAP.prompt() to enter a GAP session within OSCAR.
  5. Use the attribute system (set_attribute! / get_attribute / @attr) to cache expensive computations on objects. After having computed something costly (for instance a Gröbner basis, a Hilbert polynomical, or a singular locus), store it on the object itself: set_attribute!(I, :my_groebner, groebner_basis(I)), and then later get_attribute(I, :my_groebner). Many OSCAR functions already do this internally, and the @attr macro turns a function into an automatically memoized attribute.
